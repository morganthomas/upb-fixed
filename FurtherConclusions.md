# Further Conclusions from *UPB*: Open Work

This note tracks the conclusions Stefan Molyneux draws in *Universally Preferable Behaviour* that the theory in `UPBJurisdiction.lean` doesn't yet justify. What the theory does establish is in `Conclusions.md`, and its premises are in `Premises.md`.

Book sections are cited by their headings. Item labels (A1, F3 and so on) are kept from earlier versions of this note, so references elsewhere still work.

## Open

**F1. Threats** (*Avoidance and Initiation*; also labeled C8). The book holds that "any threat of the initiation of violence is immoral," and that threats are worse the harder they are to avoid. Payments extracted by threat and agreements made under threat are already covered as duress. What's missing is the threat itself. Since a threat is a speech act, it collides with nothing physically, so compossibility (P1) can't forbid it; a new premise is needed. Design questions under discussion, now deferred:
- Is threatening forbidden? Proposed: threatening force you'd be forbidden to use is itself forbidden.
- Does a threat make the threatener liable to defensive force? Proposed: only credible, imminent threats, with "credible and imminent" as an undefined term.
- Do threats create a debt, for example for the cost of moving away from a protection racket?

**F5. Degrees of wrongness** (*Ethics, Aesthetics and Avoidability*, *The Fourth Test*, *The Fifth Test*). Lying is worse than lateness but better than assault. Fraud is less bad than theft because it can be avoided. Victims bear some responsibility for avoidable risks, such as a wallet left on a park bench. The model only distinguishes permitted from forbidden, so this would change its structure: it needs graded wrongs.

**F6. Unenforceable "oughts," which the book calls aesthetics** (*UPB: Ethics or Aesthetics?*). Truthfulness, punctuality and politeness are "universally preferable but not enforceable." The model captures the *not enforceable* half (Theorem 7A) but has no category for the *preferable* half. In the model, lying is simply permitted. This would also change the model's structure.

**F7. Lying in defense or to a liar** (*The Fifth Test*). The book holds that lying is normally wrong but justified in defense, as when lying to someone hunting your wife. This is consistent with the model, but only trivially, since lying is always permitted there. It depends on F6.

**F9. Owning what you produce** (*Property and UPB*: "we own that which we produce"). First possession covers new things the maker possesses first. It doesn't settle making things from someone else's materials, or employment.

**F10. Advance refusals** (raised while doing F2; deferred by decision). Someone who, while capable, refuses certain help in advance (a "do not resuscitate" instruction, say) should have that refusal respected after losing capacity. The model currently gives no veto to anyone lacking capacity, including in this case.

**F8. Punishment and incarceration.** Out of scope, by decision. The book endorses restitution but not retribution, and its remark that the starving thief "must justly accept the consequences" is covered by restitution.

## Not ethical conclusions

These can't be justified by any premises of this kind.

- **Empirical claims.** Free markets are the most efficient system; state power always increases; public schools decline as force increases; nuclear deterrence keeps the peace; cultures built on coercion become more coercive; communism's failure refutes collectivist ethics; the good-versus-evil-majority argument against the state. These need evidence, not proofs.
- **Metaphysical and epistemological claims.** God doesn't exist, because "existence" means detectability; UPB validates the scientific method. These are outside ethics, and the first rests on a contested definition.
- **Part 1's "proofs" that UPB exists.** These equivocate between preferring truth in a debate and enforceable moral rules.
